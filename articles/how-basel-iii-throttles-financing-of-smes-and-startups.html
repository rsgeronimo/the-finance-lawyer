How Basel III Throttles Financing of SMEs and Startups

Banking · November 2026

A World Bank study of 32 emerging markets and developing economies found that the implementation of Basel III, the international standard on bank capital, made it harder for small and medium enterprises to obtain financing.1

This is not surprising, because the Basel framework makes it more expensive for a bank to lend to startups, SMEs, and innovative enterprises. The reason becomes clear once we consider the micro perspective of a bank.

A bank lends money that mostly belongs to other people. When it funds a loan to a business, the money comes from two sources. The first is borrowed money, which consists of deposits from the public and debt instruments such as interbank loans and bonds. The second is shareholder equity.

Deposits are the cheapest source of funds. A depositor wants safety and convenience more than yield, so an ordinary savings account at a large bank pays well under 1 percent a year.

Other debt is also relatively cheap. A bank that borrows from other banks or from the central bank pays a rate close to the policy rate, which the BSP set at 5.50 percent on 22 October 2026.2 Creditors accept these rates because they are paid ahead of the shareholders if the bank fails.

Equity is the most expensive source of funds. Shareholders are paid last, they absorb the first losses, and no one promises them a fixed return, so they expect a premium over what they could earn from a deposit or a government bond. For illustration, assume that the shareholders of a bank expect a return of 15 percent a year.

Left to itself, a bank would fund loans to borrowers with as much cheap borrowed money and as little equity as it could. The Basel framework limits this. It requires every loan to be backed by a minimum percentage of equity, and the percentage depends on the category of the borrower. In the Philippines, the full requirement is 12.5 percent of the loan, and each category of asset carries a risk weight that determines what fraction of that 12.5 percent applies.3 In plain terms, loans that regulators regard as riskier must be funded with more equity, and because equity is the most expensive source of funds, those loans cost the bank more to make.

Take a bank with PHP 100 million to deploy, and assume that its borrowed funds (deposits and debt together) cost an average of 3 percent a year while its equity costs 15 percent. The cost of funding that PHP 100 million depends on where the bank puts it.

  • Government securities need no equity backing. The bank funds the whole amount with borrowed money at a cost of PHP 3 million a year, or 3.00 percent.
  • A loan to a conglomerate with the highest credit rating needs PHP 2.5 million of equity. The funding cost rises to about PHP 3.3 million a year, or 3.30 percent.
  • A residential mortgage needs PHP 6.25 million of equity. The funding cost is PHP 3.75 million a year, or 3.75 percent.
  • A loan to a business with no credit rating needs the full PHP 12.5 million of equity. The funding cost is PHP 4.5 million a year, or 4.50 percent.

The same PHP 100 million therefore costs the bank 3.00 percent a year to fund when it is lent to the government and 4.50 percent when it is lent to an unrated business. The bank must charge that business 1.5 percentage points more before it has examined anything about the borrower itself. Nearly all startups and SMEs have no credit rating, so they fall into the last category whatever their record of repayment. A bank facing these numbers either passes the higher cost on to them or places its money in the cheaper categories, which is the result that the World Bank study found out.

The broader implication is that the Basel capital adequacy regulations throttle access to finance for SMEs, startups, and innovative enterprises. Every bank is then pushed toward government securities, conglomerates, and secured borrowers, and the credit left over for unrated businesses is smaller than it would otherwise be. The regulations also make bank finance very expensive for the enterprises that do obtain it, because the bank recovers the cost of the additional equity through a higher interest rate.
