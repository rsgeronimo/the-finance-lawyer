Why Society Must Tolerate a Certain Level of Laundered Money

Financial Regulation · November 2026

Economists have long accepted that the efficient amount of crime is above zero, because preventing the last offense costs more than the offense itself.1 Shops accept some shoplifting because searching every customer would drive customers away, and card issuers accept some fraud because approving payments quickly is worth more than the fraud costs. The same reasoning applies to laundering, since a financial system with no laundered money at all would be one in which nobody could move funds without first proving where they came from. Such a system would serve honest people very badly.

Honest people greatly outnumber criminals, hence most of the transactions that an anti-money laundering rule will curtail are legitimate ones.

A recent example comes from the Philippine central bank, which in September 2025 limited cash withdrawals from banks to PHP 500,000 a day. The measure followed an investigation into flood control projects, in which government contractors accused of corruption had taken out cash in bulk. Five months later, the BSP doubled the limit to PHP 1 million, after banks and businesses showed that a large number of legitimate transactions, such as payrolls, loan releases and project payments, exceeded the original ceiling.2

Consider, as an illustration, a provincial contractor who pays three hundred workers in cash every other Saturday because most of them have no bank account. Under the first version of the rule, he must spread the withdrawal over several days, pay by check, or submit to enhanced due diligence each time. He has done nothing wrong, and the rule costs him time every two weeks. A contractor who is stealing public funds has lawyers, several accounts, and other ways to move the money.

The same pattern appeared a year later. In September 2026, the Monetary Board blocked incoming InstaPay and PESONet transfers to the e-wallet operator of Coins.ph.3 Every user of the platform lost the ability to fund a wallet from a bank account, including people who use it to receive remittances or payment for freelance work.

Money is useful in proportion to how easily it changes hands. Every control adds a delay, a document or a refused transaction, and businesses adapt by holding more idle cash, splitting payments, or staying outside the banking system altogether.

These costs would be worth bearing if the rules stopped a lot of financial crimes, but the available estimates say that they do not. One widely cited calculation puts the amount intercepted worldwide at about USD 3 billion a year out of an estimated USD 3 trillion in criminal funds, which is one-tenth of 1 percent, while compliance costs banks and other businesses more than USD 300 billion a year.4 A recent review of the research found a consensus that the system works poorly.5

The practical question for a regulator is therefore how much dirty money can be tolerated. The BSP has warned that gambling-related transactions could return the country to the international watch list for money laundering. A regulator in that position is rewarded for performative actions since it is seldom blamed for the slowdown in the velocity of money.6
